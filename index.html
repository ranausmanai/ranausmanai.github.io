Papers

Peer-Voted LLM-Agent Stress Tests Find Feed-Induced Lexical Convergence but No Reliable Matched-Exposure Advantage for Distributed Sources
arXiv e-print, 2026 · with Dominic Williamson
A preregistered study of 448 synthetic-agent trials across four open-weight model families, testing how peer-ranked feeds affect downstream language and decisions. Peer exposure reliably increased lexical similarity, but multiple distributed sources did not produce a reliable advantage over one source when impressions were matched.
PhantomFill: When the Form Demands an Answer, Language Models Invent One
arXiv e-print, 2026 · accepted at the Stanford AIMS Workshop, 2026
This benchmark tests whether required schemas make language models invent information that is absent from the input. Required fields drove fabrication to 100% in 10 of 13 models, while an explicit escape option was largely ignored by open-weight models.
Under Pressure: Emotional Framing Induces Measurable Behavioral Shifts and Structured Internal Geometry in Small Language Models
arXiv e-print, 2026
Using small local models on impossible-constraint coding tasks, this study measures how emotional framing changes both answers and internal representations. Pressure produced the strongest shortcut behavior, while calm and curiosity preserved honesty more often; the framing directions also formed a structured geometry in the final layer.
Adversarial Feeds Steer LLM Agent Decisions Against Their Defaults
arXiv e-print, 2026
This work isolates the effect of a ranked feed by holding the agent, persona, topic, and final prompt fixed while changing only the preceding scrolling experience. Across 2,785 rollouts, adversarial feeds shifted uncertain decisions dramatically, but generally could not overturn strong defaults; two simple feed-level defenses partly reduced the effect.

About

Hi, I’m Usman, writing from Islamabad. I spend my days in the space between research and production, and honestly, I still find it a little magical. There’s a real joy in taking a fuzzy idea, wrestling it into a working system, and watching it quietly make someone’s day a bit easier.

The thread running through everything I build is simple and a little stubborn. AI should be useful, inspectable, and honest about what it can’t do. I’m not an academic in the formal sense. I’m a practitioner who got here by staying endlessly curious and never quite willing to stop poking at things. When a result feels solid enough to stand on its own, I write it up and share it in the open, hoping it saves someone else a few late nights.

Away from the keyboard, you’ll usually find me out running or horse riding, which is where most of my best thinking quietly happens. I grew up on Carl Sagan’s Cosmos and Douglas Adams in equal measure, so I’m equally moved by the thought that we’re made of star stuff and tickled that the answer to life, the universe, and everything might just be 42. I try to hold onto both: stay a little awestruck, and don’t panic.

Mostly, I just love making things and learning out loud. If any of this resonates, or you’d like to trade notes on evaluation, small models, or our shared little spot on this pale blue dot, my inbox is genuinely always open. I’d love to hear from you.

A few things I never get tired of thinking about:

  • LLM evaluation and reliability
  • Agent workflows and tool use
  • Small-model and local inference research
  • Behavioral analysis and interpretability
  • Practical AI systems under real operating constraints
